# Narrative Report on Grant Programs Mentioned in the Day-1 Proposal

_Prepared March 19, 2026_

## Purpose

This report translates the grant list in the Day-1 cluster materials into a speaking brief. It is meant to answer the follow-up question, "What exactly is that grant, why does it fit us, and how current is it?" Where the internal notes and the official program pages diverged, I treated the official page as the source of truth.

This report covers the grant programs named across the current Day-1 materials, especially:

- `cluster_presentations/cluster_plan.md`
- `cluster_presentations/grant_opportunities.md`
- `cluster_presentations/state_local_funding.md`
- `cluster_presentations/day1_slide_shell_expanded.md`

## Big Picture

The cluster's funding story is strongest when it is presented in layers rather than as a flat list.

- The best immediate named proposal is `NSF DRMS`. It is current, theory-driven, clearly relevant to risk, resilience, and decision-making, and it gives the group a believable first collective move.
- The strongest major federal scale-up path is `NSF GCR`, but it should be framed as the flagship the cluster grows toward, not as the first grant we submit.
- The strongest federal domain-specific route for rural, land-use, conservation, and resource systems work is `USDA AFRI`.
- The strongest non-federal seed or capacity-building source is `The Meadows Foundation`.
- The clearest Texas state deadline example is the `Texas GLO Coastal Management Program`.
- The best internal bridge is `UTA IRP`, because it explicitly expects a follow-on external proposal.
- Several items in the internal notes are real but should be treated as monitor or relationship items rather than confident Year 1 proposals: `HEGS`, `NEH Collaborative Research`, `FEMA BRIC`, `DOE VTO`, `RWJF`, `Kresge`, and the public-facing mechanics for `NCTCOG NTCMT`.

## Important Corrections to Existing Internal Notes

1. `NSF GCR` is not a simple one-step `$2.4M` grant. The official solicitation uses a two-phase structure: `Phase I` can be up to `$1.2M` for years 1-2, and only exceptionally strong teams can move into `Phase II` at up to `$2.4M` for years 3-5.

2. `NSF S&CC` should be described using the solicitation's recurring date structure, not a vague "`~Sep 2026`" estimate. The current solicitation uses recurring second-Monday cycles. For fall 2026, that implies a preliminary proposal on `September 14, 2026` and a full proposal target date on `November 9, 2026`.

3. `NSF HEGS` is not safe to present as an active deadline right now. The current official HEGS page says `Status: Waiting for new publication` and shows `No upcoming due dates`. The old solicitation remains visible, but the current public status says monitor the program rather than build the deck around it.

4. `NEH Collaborative Research` should not be presented with a confident future date unless NEH posts a new cycle. The current official page shows the most recent application window opened on `September 22, 2025` and closed on `December 3, 2025`. As of March 19, 2026, it is a real fit but not a current open call.

5. `FEMA BRIC` should be handled very carefully. FEMA's own April 4, 2025 press release says the agency ended the program and canceled prior BRIC applications. BRIC pages and FY2024 materials still exist on FEMA's site, but I did not verify an active FY2026 open competition.

6. `DOE VTO` is a real funder, but the current DOE funding page is still anchored around a FY2025 VTO NOFO with 2025 deadlines. That makes it a monitor item for this conversation, not a strong named Year 1 commitment.

7. `NCTCOG NTCMT` is real and regionally relevant, but the public NCTCOG materials I found do not cleanly verify the exact "rolling quarterly" mechanics or cost-share structure stated in the internal notes. I would confirm those directly with NCTCOG before stating them in the room.

## 1. NSF Decision, Risk and Management Sciences (DRMS)

`DRMS` is the cleanest first grant for the cluster because it lives exactly where risk, decision-making, public policy, and disaster response intersect. The program supports work on judgment, decision analysis, risk analysis and communication, societal and public-policy decision-making, and management science. It also explicitly supports the `RAPID` funding mechanism for time-sensitive work tied to disasters and other ephemeral events.

This matters because the Day-1 proposal already frames the cluster around resilience, environmental risk, flood exposure, public response to environmental harms, and community decision-making. That is much closer to DRMS than to a generic "sustainability" label. If someone presses on why the fit is strong, the answer is that this is not just a climate or flood topic; it is a decision-and-risk topic about how households, communities, institutions, and markets respond to environmental stress.

As of March 19, 2026, the official DRMS page is active and lists target dates of `August 18, 2026` and `January 19, 2027`. NSF does not advertise a fixed ceiling on the main program page. Our internal planning assumption of roughly `$100K-$400K` is fine as a speaking estimate, but in the room I would describe it as a mid-sized core NSF program rather than state a hard ceiling.

The best way to talk about DRMS is: "This is our best first collective federal proposal because it is current, theory-driven, and fits all four scholars without forcing an artificial technical frame." The main caution is that DRMS expects social and behavioral science grounding. A proposal that sounds like pure engineering, a map-making service project, or a local planning report would be weak.

Cluster fit is strongest for flood-risk decision making, water-quality response behavior, community risk communication, and land-use or conservation decision systems.

Official sources:

- [NSF DRMS program page](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/drms-decision-risk-management-sciences)

## 2. NOAA Broad Agency Announcement

The `NOAA Broad Agency Announcement` is a flexible NOAA mechanism rather than a single sharply themed sustainability grant. That distinction matters. It is useful because it can support research, education, outreach, or innovative projects that align with NOAA's mission but do not fit neatly into one of NOAA's narrower standing competitions.

For this cluster, NOAA is strongest when the project is clearly about coastal, hydrologic, or climate-related resilience in Texas and when the proposal can point to NOAA-relevant data, environmental systems, or hazards. It is less persuasive if it is used as a generic placeholder for any climate work. In other words, NOAA becomes convincing when the pitch is flood resilience, coastal risk, climate adaptation, or environmental monitoring, not just "sustainability" in the abstract.

As of March 19, 2026, NOAA Fisheries' open-opportunities page lists the `Fiscal Year 2024-2026 Broad Agency Announcement` as open through `September 30, 2026`. The official BAA page also notes that funding is contingent on appropriations and that publication of the announcement does not obligate NOAA to make any particular award.

The best way to talk about NOAA is as a flexible federal option that can support Pilot A or Pilot B when the work is framed around flood or climate systems in Texas. The main caution is that it is not as predictable or programmatically clean as DRMS or AFRI. It is real, but I would present it as an opportunistic NOAA route rather than as the anchor of the cluster's Year 1 plan.

Cluster fit is strongest for Andrews and Bray, especially if the proposal uses Texas Gulf Coast, inland flooding, or environmental hazard systems as the main frame.

Official sources:

- [NOAA Fisheries open funding opportunities](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/funding-opportunities/open-opportunities)
- [FY 2024-2026 NOAA Broad Agency Announcement](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/index.php/grant/fiscal-year-2024-2026-broad-agency-announcement)

## 3. USDA AFRI Foundational and Applied Science

`USDA AFRI Foundational and Applied Science` is the strongest federal route for the parts of the cluster that touch rural systems, natural resources, land use, conservation, agriculture, and environmental economics. It is a much better fit than some NSF programs when the research question turns on agricultural communities, rural resilience, land-management behavior, or environmental and natural-resource economics.

The official AFRI page is unusually helpful because it lays out the overall frame clearly. It supports six priority areas, including `Bioenergy, Natural Resources, and Environment` and `Agriculture Economics and Rural Communities`, both of which are directly relevant to the current proposal materials.

As of March 19, 2026, the official NIFA page shows the current AFRI Foundational and Applied Science competition as posted on `August 1, 2025`, with a closing date of `December 31, 2026`, an estimated total program funding level of `$300,000,000`, and an award range of `$10,000-$10,000,000`. That large range is important. It means the program is real and well-funded, but it does not mean every proposal can reasonably ask for `$10M`. Budget size depends on the specific program area and project type.

The right way to talk about AFRI is: "This is our strongest federal route for rural resilience, land and water systems, conservation economics, and community vitality in Texas." The caution is that AFRI is really a family of program areas and priorities. The team would need to target the correct sub-area rather than simply submit a broad sustainability proposal.

Cluster fit is strongest for Shost, Marable, and Andrews, with Bray contributing where community vitality, rural social systems, or participatory work matter.

Official sources:

## 4. NSF Environmental Sustainability

`NSF Environmental Sustainability` is a core CBET program and works best when the cluster's work is framed as environmental systems, sustainability engineering, or infrastructure-oriented environmental problem solving. It is not the best umbrella for the entire four-person cluster, but it is very useful for smaller collaborations or individual PI projects inside the larger cluster.

The program is attractive because it is ongoing and does not rely on one narrow annual competition window. The official NSF page presents it as an active program opportunity rather than a one-off solicitation cycle. That makes it a useful "always available" route for proposals that are mature enough to submit when ready.

The internal notes use roughly `$300K` as a planning estimate for a standard three-year project. I would keep that only as an internal heuristic. On the public side, I would say the program funds core environmental sustainability research and is especially helpful for smaller or mid-sized environmental systems proposals.

The best way to talk about Environmental Sustainability is that it gives the cluster a serious, ongoing NSF home for water-quality, infrastructure, conservation, and sustainability systems work, especially where the project leans technical or systems-oriented. The caution is that this is not a catch-all social science or humanities program. If the proposal is too far from engineering science or systems framing, it will be a weaker fit.

Cluster fit is strongest for Marable and Shost, with Andrews relevant where environmental systems, modeling, or infrastructure questions are central.

Official sources:

- [NSF Environmental Sustainability program page](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/environmental-sustainability/505695/pd20-7643)

## 5. NSF Growing Convergence Research (GCR)

`NSF GCR` is the cluster's strongest flagship funding story, but it is also the grant most likely to be oversold. The official solicitation is clear that GCR exists to cultivate convergence research that deeply integrates disciplines around a compelling problem and makes a case that the work is not already supportable through another NSF program.

That last point matters a great deal. GCR is not just a bigger interdisciplinary grant. It is for teams that can show deep conceptual integration across directorates or divisions and that the work would not fit a more ordinary NSF home. In practice, that means the cluster needs pilot data, shared methods, partner relationships, and a convincing convergence problem statement before GCR becomes credible.

As of March 19, 2026, the official GCR page lists a `February 8, 2027` deadline. It also confirms the two-phase budget structure: up to `$1.2M` for Phase I in years 1-2 and up to `$2.4M` for Phase II in years 3-5, contingent on exceptional progress after a reverse site visit. This is the most important correction to the internal notes.

The best way to speak about GCR is: "Our Year 1 proposals are designed to make a GCR-scale convergence proposal credible by February 2027." That framing is strong because it shows ambition without fantasy. The caution is that if the team presents GCR as an immediate first submission, it will sound under-developed.

Cluster fit is excellent across all four candidates if the problem is framed as equitable environmental resilience in Texas and if the cluster can demonstrate genuine methodological integration rather than parallel projects under one umbrella.

Official sources:

- [NSF GCR program page](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/gcr-growing-convergence-research)
- [NSF GCR solicitation NSF 24-527](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/gcr-growing-convergence-research/nsf24-527/solicitation)

## 6. NSF Smart and Connected Communities (S&CC)

`NSF Smart and Connected Communities` is one of the strongest scale-up routes for the cluster once a real community partnership is in place. It is especially compelling when the work connects intelligent systems, data, infrastructure, and community-defined resilience or mobility problems.

The official solicitation is more expansive than the internal shorthand suggests. It includes `Development Grants`, `Integrative Research Grants`, and `Large-Scale Research` awards. For this cluster, the realistic fit is probably an `Integrative Research Grant` rather than a large-scale award. The solicitation states that IRG proposals can go up to `$1.5M`, while large-scale awards may range from `$4M-$5M`.

The due-date structure is recurring and important. The official solicitation gives second-Monday recurring dates for both fall and spring cycles. For fall 2026, that means a preliminary proposal on `September 14, 2026` and a full proposal target date on `November 9, 2026`. That is much stronger than calling it simply "`~Sep 2026`."

The right way to talk about S&CC is: "This is our smart-resilience and infrastructure scale-up route, but it requires a real community partner and a serious evaluation plan." The caution is that S&CC is not just a technology grant. NSF expects the community to be clearly defined, meaningfully engaged, and tied to the research design. A weak partner letter or a vague "we will work with Arlington" stance would not be enough.

Cluster fit is strongest where Andrews, Marable, and Bray can combine spatial data, infrastructure, mobility, community engagement, and environmental resilience.

Official sources:

- [NSF S&CC solicitation NSF 25-527](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/scc-smart-connected-communities/505364/nsf25-527/solicitation)

## 7. NSF Human-Environment and Geographical Sciences (HEGS) / TREES

`HEGS` is one of the best intellectual fits for the cluster, but it is not one of the safest public talking points right now. The current HEGS program page says `Status: Waiting for new publication` and explicitly states `No upcoming due dates`. It supports fundamental research on human-environment and geographical sciences, including the kind of spatial, social, and environmental integration the cluster wants to showcase. But the current official page tells us not to speak as if there is a live 2026 competition with a firm deadline.

The main caution is twofold. First, do not present it as open right now. Second, even when active, HEGS is theory-building fundamental science, not just applied geospatial service work and not primarily humanistic interpretation.

Cluster fit is very strong for Andrews and also strong for Bray, Shost, and Marable where the work advances fundamental geographical or socio-environmental science.

## 9. Meadows Foundation

`The Meadows Foundation` is the strongest Texas philanthropic option in the proposal materials because it can support capacity-building, partnership formation, and other work that is often hard to fund through federal research programs. It is especially helpful for making the cluster sound rooted in Texas rather than purely federal-facing.

The official Meadows materials show that eligible applicants include qualified `501(c)(3)` organizations and public entities serving Texas. The foundation accepts proposals through an online system, allows one proposal every `12 months`, and notes a review timeline of roughly `four to five months`, with board meetings in `January`, `April`, `June`, `September`, and `November`. Meadows also emphasizes measurable outcomes, Texas benefit, and realistic organizational capacity.

That makes Meadows an excellent speaking example for shared infrastructure, community partnership development, cluster launch support, or other work that strengthens later federal applications. It is not best framed as a pure theory or academic publication funder.

The best way to talk about Meadows is: "This is the strongest non-federal seed and capacity-building source in Texas for the cluster's shared infrastructure and partnership-building work." The main caution is that the story must clearly benefit Texas and show practical value; a vague academic center proposal would be weaker than a concrete Texas-facing infrastructure and engagement pitch.

Cluster fit is broad across the whole group.

The official TWDB pages show the state funding research in topics such as artificial intelligence in flood risk assessment, stormwater asset management, flood easements, flood communication, agricultural flood loss, rainfall frequency grids, and nature-based flood mitigation.

The best way to talk about TWDB is: "Texas already funds flood-priority research on exactly the kinds of questions this cluster wants to address, and we would position ourselves for that ecosystem early." That is accurate and useful.

## 13. TCEQ Section 319(h) Nonpoint Source

`TCEQ Section 319(h)` is one of the strongest Texas water-quality pathways in the materials, especially for work that links pollution, watersheds, exposure, and community impact. The official TCEQ page makes the structure very clear: these grants are funded under `Section 319(h)` of the Clean Water Act, reimburse `60%` of project costs, require `40%` match, and are available to universities and other public entities in Texas.

As of March 19, 2026, TCEQ states that the `FY26` submittal period is closed and that the `next RFGA will open in the summer of 2026`. That is a useful level of specificity for the cluster's planning.

The right way to talk about this program is as a very practical applied research and implementation route for watershed-based planning, nonpoint source pollution reduction, and water-quality work with public partners. It is especially persuasive when tied to a concrete watershed or community-based planning effort.

The caution is that this is not a basic theory grant. It is geared toward water-quality improvement, watershed protection plans, and related applied projects. If the cluster wants pure conceptual research, this is not the right pitch.

Cluster fit is strongest for Marable, Andrews, and Bray.

## 18. DOE Vehicle Technologies Office (VTO)

`DOE VTO` is a real federal transportation and electrification funder and is worth keeping in the long-range portfolio, especially for EV, mobility, and charging-infrastructure work. But it should not be treated as a clean current anchor for the Day-1 conversation.

The official DOE funding page currently shows the `Fiscal Year 2025 Vehicle Technologies Office Program Wide Funding Opportunity Announcement`, with a concept paper deadline of `April 1, 2025` and a full application deadline of `June 18, 2025`. The DOE notice page also frames the opportunity around advanced vehicle research, mobility solutions, and transportation-related energy and infrastructure challenges.

That makes the correct interpretation pretty straightforward. DOE VTO is absolutely a legitimate future path for the cluster, but as of March 19, 2026 the official page is still anchored to a 2025 competition rather than a fresh named open 2026 opportunity. So it is better described as a monitor item.

The best way to talk about DOE VTO is: "For EV and mobility work, DOE is a serious future pathway we would monitor closely, but we would not present it as one of the clearest currently active Year 1 opportunities unless a new NOFO opens." That protects credibility.

Cluster fit is strongest for Marable and for mobility-related collaborations with Andrews.

## 19. Robert Wood Johnson Foundation (RWJF)

`RWJF` is best treated as a thematic foundation ecosystem rather than as one stable standing grant the cluster can name with high confidence. The fit is real because RWJF funds work around health equity, community conditions, and systems-level determinants of health, which can intersect with environmental justice, water, infrastructure, and climate exposure.

The official RWJF active-opportunities page confirms that opportunities are dynamic and change over time. As of March 19, 2026, one active example is `Learning from Abroad to Reimagine Health Knowledge Systems for Equity and Wellbeing`, due `April 13, 2026`. RWJF's `Exploring Equitable Futures` pathway is also relevant conceptually, but the current page notes the 2025 cycle closed on `October 15, 2025` and generally reopens around `February 1`.

That means RWJF is real and useful, but it should not be presented as if there is one simple standing "RWJF Health Equity" grant waiting for the cluster. It is more accurate to present RWJF as a high-value foundation space to monitor for environment-health-equity opportunities.

The best way to talk about RWJF is: "If the cluster develops a strong environmental-health-equity frame, RWJF is a real foundation ecosystem to watch, but the exact opportunities shift over time." That is both honest and strategically smart.

Cluster fit is strongest for Bray and Marable, with Andrews relevant when hazard exposure or infrastructure affects health and wellbeing.

## 20. Kresge Environment Program

`Kresge` is another foundation that belongs in the cluster's medium-term strategy, but not as a confident named Year 1 proposal. The fit is strong because Kresge's Environment Program works on equitable climate action in cities and highlights three initiatives that map well to the current proposal: `Climate Resilient and Equitable Water Systems (CREWS)`, `Equitable and Just Energy Transition (EJET)`, and `Climate Change, Health and Equity (CCHE)`.

The official Kresge materials also make an important process point: the Environment Program does `not accept unsolicited proposals`. That means Kresge is fundamentally a relationship and open-RFP funder, not a general anytime application route like Meadows or NSF core programs.

This distinction is very important in the room. The current proposal materials are correct to name Kresge as a relevant funder, but it should be described as a strategic foundation relationship and watchlist, not as a concrete Year 1 submission.

The best way to talk about Kresge is: "Kresge's environment initiatives are a strong thematic fit for water systems, energy transition, and climate-health-equity work, but because the program does not accept unsolicited proposals, we would treat it as a relationship and RFP-monitoring pathway." That line is accurate and sophisticated.

Cluster fit is strongest for Bray, Marable, and Andrews depending on whether the emphasis is water, energy, or climate-health-equity.

## Programs Mentioned in Internal Background Notes but Not Central to the Day-1 Deck

Several items in the supporting notes are worth knowing about, but they should not be given the same rhetorical weight as the programs above.

- `UT System STARs / Rising STARs` are institutional recruitment and startup-support mechanisms, not external collaborative research grants. They matter in negotiation and capacity planning, not as Day-1 cluster proposals.
- `GURI` is a major Texas incentive program, but it is aimed at distinguished-recruit cases and is not really a fit for the current cluster hires as described.
- `TPWD Wildlife Research` is a real state research pathway, but it is secondary unless the cluster sharpens a wildlife or habitat-management direction.
- `NCTCOG RISE Coalition` is a network and visibility platform, not a grant program.
- `USGS` appears once in the faculty-specific grant map, but the current proposal materials do not develop a concrete USGS competition or mechanism. I would not cite it in live conversation without further work.
- `GLO Resilient Communities Program`, `GLO Oil Spill R&D`, `TWDB Groundwater Grants`, `EPA environmental justice grants`, `NSF CIVIC Innovation Challenge`, `NSF HDBE`, `NSF SAI`, and `HNDS-I` are either closed, archived, repealed, reorganized, or otherwise too unstable to use as public-facing anchors without fresh re-verification.

## Short Talking Points to Memorize

- `DRMS` is our best first cluster proposal because it is active, theory-driven, and directly supports risk, resilience, and decision-making work.
- `GCR` is our flagship aspiration grant, but it is a two-phase program; Year 1 proposals are meant to make a February 2027 GCR submission credible.
- `AFRI` is our best federal route for rural resilience, conservation economics, land use, and natural-resource systems in Texas.
- `Meadows` is the strongest Texas non-federal source for seed funding, shared infrastructure, and partnership building.
- `GLO CMP` is the clearest state deadline example we can name right now.
- `S&CC` is a serious scale-up route if we have a real community partner and a smart infrastructure or resilience problem.
- `UTA IRP` is the internal bridge that helps create pilot data and forces an external interdisciplinary proposal.
- `HEGS` is a strong intellectual fit, but NSF currently lists it as waiting for a new publication.
- `BRIC` is not a dependable Year 1 plan and would require a government applicant even if it reopens.
- `DOE VTO`, `RWJF`, and `Kresge` are best treated as monitored or relationship-based opportunities rather than primary Day-1 anchors.

## Bottom Line

The cluster's funding portfolio is strong, but the programs do not all belong in the same rhetorical category. The most defensible public story is:

- `DRMS` first.
- `AFRI`, `Meadows`, `GLO`, `Environmental Sustainability`, and `UTA IRP` as credible supporting near-term routes.
- `S&CC` and `GCR` as the scale-up logic.
- `HEGS`, `NEH`, `BRIC`, `DOE`, `RWJF`, `Kresge`, and some of the regional pathways as monitor or relationship items.

That structure gives you a much more confident answer if someone says, "Tell me more about the grants you named," because it distinguishes between the opportunities we can speak about as concrete next steps and the ones we should present as informed longer-range possibilities.
